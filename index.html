Focus

About

I am a phonetician and laboratory phonologist who studies prosody. My work is motivated by fundamental questions about how speakers use prosody to convey meaning and organize utterances. My research questions vary from: How do speakers highlight new, important, or contrastive information (i.e., mark focus) in an utterance? How do they organize speech into cognitive constituents, and do the prosodic boundaries of these constituents interact with focus marking, revealing patterns of speech planning? How is linguistic rhythm encoded across different types of word-level (e.g., lexical stress, lexical pitch accent, lexical tone, or no lexical prosody) and phrase-level (e.g., head- or edge-prominence) prominence systems?

In extending these questions to second language (L2) contexts, I ask how prosody—at both the word and phrase level—is acquired in L2 speech, including whether more salient aspects (e.g., nuclear accents) are acquired earlier than others (e.g., lack of polysyllabic shortening), and to what extent the prosodic patterns observed in L2 production reflect first language (L1) transfer versus general developmental trajectories shared across diverse L1 backgrounds.

I approach these questions using a wide range of methods, including articulatory data from Electromagnetic Articulography (EMA), acoustic analyses, corpus-based studies, and perception experiments. By addressing these questions across typologically diverse languages, my work aims not only to document the prosodic structure of different languages, but to explain why such structures emerge, what cognitive and motor mechanisms give rise to them, and how prosody functions as a core component of both grammar and human communication

Before UCSB, I completed an MA in Linguistics at National Taiwan University and a BA in English at National Kaohsiung Normal University, where I trained and worked as an EFL teacher — a thread that still runs through my interest in second-language prosody.

Program

Research

My research asks how speakers use prosody to mark focus, organize utterances into constituents, and build linguistic rhythm — and how those systems are acquired in a second language. Four threads run through this work.

Focus and the Tone–Intonation Interaction
In tonal languages, pitch already carries lexical meaning, so marking focus means layering phrase-level intonation on top of lexical tone without erasing it. Using EMA data from Taiwan Mandarin, I ask whether focus is a distinct layer of structure or an emergent effect of tone-intonation interaction, and whether the hierarchy of focus types documented in stress languages like English and German also holds in a tone language.
Prominence and Boundary as a Unified System
Prominence and boundary marking are usually treated as separate parts of prosody. A second EMA study of Taiwan Mandarin tests whether they instead arise from one coupled dynamical system — whether the location of focus shapes where a prosodic boundary falls, and whether boundary placement can itself signal focus.
Linguistic Rhythm
With Argyro Katsika and Matthew Gordon, I'm re-examining the classic stress-timed / syllable-timed rhythm typology across 272 genetically and areally diverse languages. The pattern so far: rhythm isn't set by any single level like the syllable or foot, but by how syllable, foot, word, and phrase interact. Related EMA work traces how moraic structure governs phrase-final lengthening in Tokyo Japanese, and how vowel length and metrical structure interact in Estonian.
Second Language Prosody
How do L1 Taiwan Mandarin speakers acquire the prosody of L2 English — and which patterns are L1 transfer versus a more general developmental path? Mandarin speakers tend to mark focus in English by lengthening and raising pitch across the whole word rather than just the stressed syllable, a signature of L1 transfer. Meanwhile, accent acquisition follows a broader trajectory: salient, categorical features like nuclear accents are learned before finer phonetic detail like pitch alignment.
Looking ahead Building the articulatory evidence for how tone and intonation combine, testing the prominence–boundary hypothesis directly, and connecting prosodic typology to L2 learning trajectories — eventually extending into discourse, turn-taking, and naturalistic conversation using EMA, ultrasound, EGG, and eye-tracking.
